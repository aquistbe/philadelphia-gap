The signature: speed, flight, arterials

The circumstances of a Philadelphia traffic death look like a design problem, and they moved that way after 2020: faster, darker, more often on a principal arterial, and more often with the driver gone before police arrive. Each dumbbell runs from the 2015–19 share to the 2020–24 share.

Criterion: share of that city’s traffic deaths carrying each flag. Deaths by city: Philadelphia 485 → 712; New York 1,067 → 1,171; Chicago 652 → 940; Washington 136 → 196; Boston 108 → 120. Flags are crash-level, so every death in a crash carries that crash’s flags; intervals use an effective sample size that accounts for multi-fatality crashes.

Philadelphia moved on four of the five. Speeding involvement rose 8.4 points, hit-and-run 6.8, darkness 6.8 and principal-arterial share 6.2, each outside its margin of error. Alcohol involvement did not move (13.0% → 12.4%). The rises are not all Philadelphia’s alone: hit-and-run rose by a similar amount in New York and Chicago, so driver flight after 2020 is a shared phenomenon rather than a local one. Speeding is the sharper contrast — it rose in Philadelphia and New York while falling in Chicago and Washington. Boston moved on nothing; with about 110 deaths per period its intervals are roughly ±12 points, so read it as uninformative rather than as stability.

Two rows carry measurement warnings. Chicago’s alcohol share falls from 21.5% to 6.5%, and Washington’s principal-arterial share rises from 15.4% to 38.3% — both far larger than any plausible behavioural change and most likely reflecting how those agencies recorded blood alcohol and roadway class. Philadelphia’s own state records tell the same cautionary story: PennDOT’s recorded alcohol involvement in fatal crashes fell from 26.6% to 17.3% over the same period while the federal imputed-BAC measure charted here stayed flat, which is what a change in testing and reporting looks like rather than a change in drinking. One Philadelphia figure the federal data cannot show: 74% of the city’s fatal crashes in 2020–24 were on state-owned roads, essentially unchanged from 73% in 2015–19 (PennDOT roadway ownership).

Source: FARS 2015–2024, crashes assigned to cities by point-in-polygon against TIGER 2024 place boundaries. Two boulevards

Roosevelt Boulevard carried a fifth of Philadelphia’s traffic deaths in 2018. After automated speed enforcement went live in 2020 its share fell to 3.6% by 2024. Broad Street, without cameras for the whole period, moved the other way.

Criterion: each corridor’s deaths as a share of all Philadelphia traffic deaths that year, 2015–2024. Philadelphia only.

Roosevelt Boulevard speed cameras began issuing citations in 2020 (marked). Broad Street cameras were authorised later and began operating in November 2025 — after this series ends, so no effect can appear here.

Where, before and after

Each dot is a fatal crash. Slide between the two periods. At census-tract level the death rate rises monotonically with poverty — 9.3 per 100,000 per year in the poorest quartile of tracts in 2020–24, against 5.0 in the richest — and the gap widened after 2020 (rate ratio 1.5 → 1.8 in this PennDOT series; 1.65 → 1.99 in the FARS version with corridor deaths reassigned). The visible belt runs from Hunting Park east through Juniata Park to Kensington and south through Tioga, Allegheny West and Hartranft — the neighborhoods Roosevelt Boulevard, Hunting Park Avenue, Allegheny Avenue and Kensington Avenue pass through.

Criterion: PennDOT fatal crashes at crash location, 2015–2019 (483 deaths) vs 2020–2024 (714 deaths). Who bears it

Traffic death rates in Philadelphia rise step by step as neighborhood income falls. In 2020–24 the poorest quarter of census tracts lost residents to traffic crashes at twice the rate of the richest quarter — and the gap is wider than it was before 2020, because the post-pandemic surge landed hardest on the poorest tracts.

Where the corridor deaths belong

Criterion: the 234 deaths on roads in tracts with no resident population (parkways, industrial and airport arterials, the stadium district), each assigned to the nearest populated tract measured from the crash point — at most 2.3 km away.

Nearly half of these deaths — 47% against the 25% a neutral distribution would give — occur beside the poorest quarter of the city, and 71% beside the poorer half. Philadelphia’s high-speed corridors run along its low-income neighborhoods, so excluding those deaths does not make the analysis conservative; it removes the burden from the neighborhoods that carry it.

Methods & caveats

City assignment

A crash is assigned to a city when FARS codes it inside that city’s incorporated boundary. Municipal boundaries are not comparable units of urbanism: Phoenix and Jacksonville annex large low-density areas; Boston and Washington do not. Rankings across the 40 largest cities should be read with that in mind, and are one reason Philadelphia’s national rank (30th) and its rank among dense peers (worst of five) disagree.

Denominators

All rates are deaths per 100,000 resident population per year, using Census Bureau estimates for the mid-point year of each window. Resident population is a proxy for exposure, not a measure of it: it does not count commuters, visitors, or vehicle miles travelled, and it understates exposure in cities with large daytime populations. Multi-year windows use person-years (population × years).

Intervals

Confidence intervals are exact Poisson 95% intervals on the count, rescaled to the rate. They express counting variability only. They do not cover error in the population denominator, boundary assignment, or FARS coding, all of which are larger than the interval for the small cities.

Preliminary and provisional data

The FARS 2024 annual file is preliminary and is revised upward in most years as late cases are adjudicated; the 2024 figures here will likely rise slightly. The 2025 Philadelphia figure (~100) comes from PennDOT and is provisional. It is drawn from a different collection system than the FARS series and is not directly comparable to the bars beside it, though it is the best available indication of direction.

Municipal feeds are not cross-comparable

City dashboards count different things on different clocks. They are useful for tracking a single city within itself and useless for comparing cities. The counts below are shown only to make that concrete — do not build a comparison from them.

Framing

These are counts of deaths produced by a road system. Speed, roadway cross-section, lighting, and enforcement coverage are design and policy variables; the flags in “the signature” describe crash circumstances, not culpability. Nothing here supports a claim about individual behaviour, and the equity panel describes where crashes happen, not who is at fault.
